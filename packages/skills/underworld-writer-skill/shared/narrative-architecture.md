# Narrative Architecture: Scene Structure & Pacing for True Crime

## The Dramatization vs. Summary Decision

Not every fact becomes a scene. Use this matrix to decide:

| Element | Dramatize | Summary |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| Crime discovery (arrest warrant served, hack detected) | ✓ | – |
| Suspect interrogation (core investigative moment) | ✓ | – |
| Financial transaction (one of hundreds) | – | ✓ |
| Trial verdict | ✓ | – |
| Appeals (procedural, not contested) | – | ✓ |
| Conspiracy recruitment (establishes relationship) | ✓ | – |
| Routine marketplace activity | – | ✓ |

**Rule:** Dramatize only moments that reveal character, establish capability, or turn the investigation. Summarize procedural or repetitive elements.

---

## Scene Structure: The 5-Element Frame

Every dramatized scene needs:

1. **Specificity** (location, time, sensory detail)
   - ✓ "At 3 AM, the server room was silent except for the hum of cooling fans"
   - ✗ "It was dark and quiet"

2. **Sourced Action** (what the scene shows must come from a documented source)
   - ✓ "The indictment described Johnson accessing the server from an IP in Pennsylvania"
   - ✗ "Johnson nervously glanced over his shoulder as he typed"
   
   **Exception:** Dramatized dialogue is permitted *only* if:
   - It appears in interview transcripts (Tier 2+)
   - It's paraphrased, not verbatim-invented
   - The original source is attributed in-text or in notes

3. **Perspective** (whose viewpoint is this?)
   - Third-person limited to investigator: ✓ (investigator's observation)
   - Third-person limited to suspect: ✓ *only* if suspect interview (Tier 2)
   - Omniscient narration of suspect's thoughts: ✗ (unsourced interiority)

4. **Time anchoring** (when did this happen?)
   - Link to an indictment date, arrest date, or interview account
   - Example: "According to the affidavit dated March 15, 2004, Johnson logged in at 11:47 PM"

5. **Source attribution** (where does this come from?)
   - In-text: "The FBI report described the scene as…"
   - Footnote/endnote: Link to PACER docket or DOJ press release
   - Author's note: General sourcing methodology for recurring actions

## Character Development in True Crime

True crime characters are *people*, not archetypes. Show growth, contradiction, and change:

- **At crime's start:** Capability + motivation
- **During crime:** Escalation + adaptation
- **At discovery:** Reaction + choice (fight, flight, cooperate)
- **At trial:** Acceptance or denial
- **Post-release:** Redemption or recidivism

Use dialogue (from interviews), action (from court records), and indirect characterization (what others say about them) to build this arc.

## Pacing: The Three-Chapter Rhythm

### Opening (Chapters 1–3)
- **Goal:** Establish the crime, the perpetrator's capability, the stakes
- **Structure:** Hook with crime discovery → Introduce victim/investigator → Establish suspect
- **Pacing:** Quick; 2–3 shorter scenes per chapter
- **Example (Brett Johnson):** Chapter 1 opens with "How does a 16-year-old breach a credit card database?" → Chapter 2 shows ShadowCrew formation → Chapter 3: first major marketplace transaction

### Middle (Chapters 4–18)
- **Goal:** Build complexity; show how the crime scaled; develop adversaries
- **Structure:** Investigation deepens → Suspect expands operation → Law enforcement closes in
- **Pacing:** Variable; allow longer scenes where stakes clarify (police interview, arrest, trial motion)
- **Example:** Chapters showing ShadowCrew growth, carder forums, stolen-data auctions, competing hackers, law enforcement task force formation

### Resolution (Chapters 19–27)
- **Goal:** Climax (arrest/capture), trial, sentencing, aftermath
- **Structure:** Arrest or forced exit → Legal proceedings → Consequences
- **Pacing:** Build momentum in arrest scenes; slow down for trial complexity; resolve quickly after sentencing
- **Example:** ShadowCrew takedown (Chapter 19), trial/sentencing (Chapters 20–24), Johnson's post-release life (Chapters 25–27)

## Dialogue: The Three Rules

1. **No invented dialogue.** Full stop.
   - Not permissible: "Johnson told his friend, 'I'm going to hack that card processor.'"
   - Permissible: "In interviews, Johnson described his motivation: 'I wanted to see if I could do it.'" ← Tier 2 source

2. **Paraphrase dialogue from interviews is OK.**
   - Paraphrase: "Johnson recounted how he'd approached the server, thinking he could bypass the authentication layer."
   - Source: "In his interview with FBI agents, Johnson explained his approach…"

3. **Courtroom dialogue comes from transcripts.**
   - If trial transcript is public (PACER), dialogue is Tier 1 ✓
   - If dialogue is paraphrased from news account of trial, it's Tier 3

## Thematic Framing

True crime works often organize around a theme, not just chronology.

### Example Themes

- **Power & Access:** How did a teenager gain access to billion-dollar systems?
- **Community & Crime:** How did hackers form a criminal organization?
- **Detection & Capture:** How did law enforcement close the net?
- **Redemption & Recidivism:** What happens after prison?

### How to Implement

1. **Open with theme question:** "What makes someone a criminal?"
2. **Chapters answer theme through evidence:** Each investigation stage clarifies motives, capability, choices
3. **Close with thematic resolution:** Does the criminal reform? Does the system work?

Example outline:
- **Theme:** "Teenage hacker becomes crime boss; crime boss becomes informant"
- **Act 1:** Hacker's origin story (why he had the skills)
- **Act 2:** Crime boss's organization (how he scaled his operation)
- **Act 3:** Informant's redemption (cooperation with FBI, new identity)

## Reconstruction & Speculation: Transparency Rules

If you reconstruct a scene based on *patterns* (not a single source account), mark it:

**In-text:**
"Based on forensic analysis of the server logs, investigators reconstructed Johnson's approach: first, he probed the authentication layer for 37 minutes; then, he deployed a custom script."

**Author's note:**
"Chapters 5–7 reconstruct ShadowCrew's marketplace operations based on FBI affidavits and forum archives recovered during the takedown. The specific dialogue and moment-to-moment pacing are inferred from logs and transaction records, not from eyewitness accounts."

**Speculation you cannot do:**
- "Johnson probably felt nervous" ← Unsourced emotion
- "Johnson intended to evade law enforcement" ← Inference without explicit statement
- "Johnson enjoyed the power" ← Speculation (unless Tier 2 interview quotes him saying so)

**Speculation you CAN do with caveats:**
- "Some investigators believed Johnson had insider help, though the evidence was circumstantial."
- "It's possible the server was compromised months before detection, but the exact entry point remains unknown."

Use *hedging language* for analysis that goes beyond sourced fact.

## Scene Checklist Before Submission

- [ ] **Specificity:** Does the scene have sensory details (time, place, sound, atmosphere)?
- [ ] **Source:** Can I point to a Tier 1–3 source for every major claim?
- [ ] **Attribution:** Is the source explicitly named or footnoted?
- [ ] **Dialogue:** Is dialogue either verbatim-transcribed or marked as paraphrase/reconstruction?
- [ ] **Interiority:** Have I avoided inventing the suspect's thoughts or feelings?
- [ ] **Perspective:** Am I consistent about whose viewpoint this is (investigator, victim, suspect)?
- [ ] **Pacing:** Does this scene move the narrative forward or just report facts?

**If you check a box "no," rewrite the scene before submission.**
