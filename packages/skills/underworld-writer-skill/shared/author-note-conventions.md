# Author's Note Conventions: Transparent Disclosure Templates

Your author's note is the reader's contract. It explains what's sourced, what's reconstructed, and what's missing.

---

## Template 1: Sourcing Methodology

Use this when your manuscript combines multiple tiers of sources:

```
AUTHOR'S NOTE

This narrative is built from public court records, law enforcement documents, 
and direct interviews with key participants. I have organized my sources into 
three tiers:

TIER 1 (Primary Legal Record): Federal indictments, sentencing orders, and trial 
transcripts from the U.S. District Court for the Middle District of Florida (PACER 
docket 03-CR-0322). These documents are publicly available and constitute the factual 
foundation of this account.

TIER 2 (Direct Participant Account): Recorded interviews with Brett Johnson, including 
the Darknet Diaries podcast series (Ep. 128–129), in which Johnson reflects on his role 
in ShadowCrew, his methods, and his eventual cooperation with the FBI. These interviews 
provide first-person context and motivation.

TIER 3 (Journalistic Reporting): Published accounts from Wired, Ars Technica, 
KrebsOnSecurity, and other technology publications that covered the ShadowCrew case 
at the time of takedown. These sources provide contemporary context and investigative 
framing but are less authoritative than court records or direct interviews.

Where sources conflict, I have prioritized Tier 1 over Tier 2 and 3. Where gaps exist 
in the public record, I have marked them explicitly so readers can distinguish verified 
fact from reconstructed narrative.
```

## Template 2: Reconstructed Scenes (Observable Actions Only)

Use this when you describe actions documented in logs, exhibits, or forensic evidence.

**CRITICAL RULE:** Reconstruct only observable *actions*, never dialogue or internal thoughts:

```
NARRATIVE RECONSTRUCTION

Chapters 5–7 reconstruct ShadowCrew's marketplace operations based on FBI affidavits, 
server logs recovered during the 2004 takedown, and transaction records entered into 
evidence at trial. The observable actions described—login timestamps, database accesses, 
file transfers—are drawn directly from forensic evidence.

For example, Chapter 6 states: "Between March 1 and March 8, 2003, Johnson accessed 
the database four times, each session lasting approximately 45 minutes." This detail 
comes directly from the FBI Affidavit (PACER, case 03-CR-0322) and represents 
documented fact.

However, this account does NOT include:
- Dialogue (fabricated from log patterns) — dialogue must come from interviews or court records
- Internal thoughts (invented from timestamps) — thoughts require the subject's own words
- Inferred motives (guessed from activity) — motives must be stated on record by the subject

VIOLATION: Do not write "Johnson must have been frustrated when the query failed" based 
on a log showing a failed database access. Timestamps show what happened; only sources 
show why or what someone thought.

Readers should understand that log-based scenes represent the objective sequence of events 
but exclude subjective experience. If the perpetrator's thoughts matter, cite an interview.
```

## Template 3: Handling Gaps

Use this when you've exhaustively searched for something and found nothing:

```
GAPS IN THE PUBLIC RECORD

Despite systematic research, the following details remain unavailable:

• Brett Johnson's employment history prior to founding ShadowCrew (2002): I searched 
PACER, LinkedIn historical archives, and state employment records but found no public 
documentation of his pre-hacking career. This gap affects how readers can understand 
his transition into cybercrime.

• The specific identities of several ShadowCrew members mentioned in the indictment: 
The court documents refer to co-conspirators by online aliases only. While some have 
been publicly identified through journalist reporting, others remain anonymous in all 
available sources. I have identified these individuals by their aliases and noted the 
limitation.

• Johnson's incarceration experience (2004–2007): No published interviews, memoirs, or 
autobiographical accounts exist from Johnson's time in federal prison. I have used his 
post-release interviews to infer conditions but cannot provide first-hand description 
of his daily experience.

These gaps are not failures of reporting; they are limitations of the public record. 
Readers should understand that this narrative is complete only where law enforcement 
and journalism have documented it.
```

## Template 4: Victim & Witness Privacy

Use this when you've decided to protect privacy:

```
PRIVACY & PROTECTION

The millions of cardholders whose data was stolen by ShadowCrew are represented in this 
narrative by their numbers and their documented losses, not by individual case studies. 
While some victims have spoken publicly about their experiences (and are named with 
permission), most remain anonymous in court records and have not consented to public 
identification.

Minor witnesses and victims in this account are identified by initials or pseudonyms 
derived from court documents (e.g., "T.M." for Victim No. 4). This approach honors 
victim privacy while maintaining the factual integrity of the account.

Johnson's family members are mentioned only where their actions appear in the public 
record (court testimony, news reporting) or where they have granted interviews. No 
invented details about family members are included.
```

## Template 5: No Composite Characters

Use this if your manuscript contains only real, individually sourced individuals:

```
CHARACTERS & IDENTITIES

Every person named in this narrative is a real individual who played a documented role 
in the ShadowCrew case or its investigation. No composite characters have been created; 
no individuals have been merged or fictionalized. Where individuals are known by 
multiple names or aliases, I identify them as such and clarify their true identity 
upon first introduction.

For example, Brett Johnson is introduced as "Porkchop," the online alias by which he 
was known in the carder underground. His real name is provided immediately, and both 
identities are used throughout to reflect how he moved between worlds.

This approach ensures that readers understand they are reading about real people with 
documented histories, not fictionalized versions of true events.
```

## Template 6: Dialoguing Sourcing

Use this if your manuscript includes dialogue from interviews or trial transcripts:

```
DIALOGUE & QUOTES

Direct quotations in this narrative come from three sources:

1. Court transcripts and legal documents (Tier 1): Dialogue from trial transcripts, 
plea hearings, and sworn statements appears in quotation marks and is cited to the 
relevant PACER docket and date. This dialogue is verbatim from the public record.

2. Recorded interviews (Tier 2): Brett Johnson's reflections on his actions come from 
Darknet Diaries episodes 128–129, which I have listened to in full and cited by timestamp. 
This dialogue is his own account, rendered verbatim from the recording.

3. Journalist reporting (Tier 3): Paraphrased dialogue from news articles and profiles 
is attributed in-text to the journalist's reporting and the original publication. This 
dialogue is not verbatim but represents what the journalist reported the subject or 
witness said.

No dialogue has been invented. If a scene contains dialogue, it comes from a documented 
source, or the scene is marked as a narrative reconstruction without dialogue.
```

## Template 7: Speculative Passages

Use this if your manuscript includes hedged analysis or speculation:

```
ANALYSIS & SPECULATION

This narrative contains some analytical passages where I describe what investigators 
believed, what patterns suggest, or what evidence implies—but without claiming certainty. 
These passages use hedging language ("some evidence suggests," "investigators believed," 
"it's likely that") to signal that I am offering analysis, not established fact.

For example, in Chapter 12, I write: "Some investigators suspected Johnson had insider 
help within the financial institution, though direct evidence of collusion was never 
found." This reflects the investigative theory at the time but not a proven fact. 
Readers should understand the distinction.

All factual claims remain grounded in Tier 1–3 sources. Speculative analysis is clearly 
labeled as such and is never presented as fact.
```

## Template 8: Updates & Corrections

Use this if your book will be updated or if you're aware of potential factual disputes:

```
CORRECTIONS & UPDATES

This manuscript was completed in [month/year] based on publicly available sources current 
as of that date. If new documents are released, interviews become available, or facts 
are disputed, readers are encouraged to:

1. Check for updates on [your website / publisher's website]
2. Consult PACER directly (case 03-CR-0322) for original documents
3. Listen to Darknet Diaries Ep. 128–129 for first-hand accounts
4. Read journalist reporting cited in the notes for contemporary context

I retain the right to issue corrections or updates if new sources materially change 
the factual record.
```

## Example: Complete Author's Note (Brett Johnson)

```
AUTHOR'S NOTE

This narrative is drawn from three categories of sources: federal court records 
(PACER docket 03-CR-0322, Middle District of Florida), direct participant interviews 
(Darknet Diaries Ep. 128–129), and contemporary journalist reporting (Wired, Ars 
Technica, KrebsOnSecurity, 2003–2005).

All dialogue comes from documented sources: direct quotations from trial transcripts 
are cited to PACER; Brett Johnson's reflections come from his Darknet Diaries interview; 
paraphrased statements are attributed to the journalist reporting them.

Chapters 5–7 reconstruct ShadowCrew's marketplace operations from server logs, transaction 
records, and forensic evidence recovered during the 2004 takedown. These reconstructions 
are factually grounded in evidence but dramatized for narrative flow.

Where the public record is silent—such as Johnson's employment before ShadowCrew or his 
experience in prison—I have marked these gaps explicitly with [GAP — searched, not public]. 
Readers should understand that this narrative is complete only where law enforcement, 
courts, and journalism have documented it.

No composite characters appear in this account. Every named individual is real and played 
a documented role in the ShadowCrew case or its investigation.

Victim privacy has been honored by using court-assigned identifiers (e.g., "T.M." for 
named victims) rather than full identities, except where victims have publicly consented 
to identification.

---

This note tells readers: what you're reading is sourced, verified, and transparent about 
its gaps and limitations.
```

## Checklist Before Writing Your Author's Note

- [ ] Have I identified my three source categories (Tier 1, 2, 3)?
- [ ] Have I explained why I chose my hierarchy (why is Tier 1 more authoritative)?
- [ ] Have I disclosed any reconstructed scenes and their basis?
- [ ] Have I listed known gaps and explained my search protocol?
- [ ] Have I confirmed that no composite characters exist in my manuscript?
- [ ] Have I addressed victim privacy and naming conventions?
- [ ] Have I clarified dialogue sourcing for the three tiers?
- [ ] Have I noted any speculative or hedged passages?
- [ ] Is the tone confident but honest—not defensive?

**Your author's note is not an apology. It's a warranty. Write accordingly.**
